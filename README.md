## 1. Protein Information ##

This laboratory activity focused on the sequence analysis of human alpha-L-iduronidase (IDUA), a lysosomal enzyme involved in the degradation of glycosaminoglycans. IDUA plays an important role in lysosomal digestion by removing terminal alpha-L-iduronic acid residues from dermatan sulfate and heparan sulfate. This process allows these complex molecules to undergo further breakdown inside lysosomes and prevents their abnormal accumulation within cells.

Proper IDUA activity is necessary for normal lysosomal function and the recycling of complex cellular materials.

### 2.3. Identification of Potential N-Glycosylation Motifs ##

The IDUA protein sequence was examined for potential N-glycosylation motifs using the N-X-S/T sequence pattern. In this pattern, N represents asparagine, X represents any standard amino acid except proline, and S/T represents serine or threonine.

**Table 4. Potential N-Glycosylation Motifs in the Human IDUA Protein**

The exact number, positions, and sequences of the potential N-glycosylation motifs are determined by R and saved in `idua_nglycosylation_motifs.csv`.

Previous structural research identified six N-linked glycosylation sites in human IDUA at asparagine residues 110, 190, 336, 372, 415, and 451 (Maita et al., 2013). These experimentally studied sites provide reference points for interpreting the sequence analysis. However, the number of sequence motifs detected by R may differ from the number of sites confirmed to carry glycans.

## 3. Questions and Answers ##

### 1. What does your enzyme normally break down inside lysosomes?

Alpha-L-iduronidase (IDUA) is a lysosomal enzyme involved in the degradation of dermatan sulfate and heparan sulfate, which are types of glycosaminoglycans. It removes terminal alpha-L-iduronic acid residues, allowing other lysosomal enzymes to continue breaking down these molecules. This process is essential for normal lysosomal catabolism and the recycling of cellular materials (Maita et al., 2013).

### 2. What is the total length of your protein, and which amino acids are most common?

The human IDUA protein represented by NCBI RefSeq accession NP_000194.2 consists of 653 amino acids. The five most abundant amino acids and their percentages were determined using R. Their exact counts and percentages are reported in the generated amino acid composition table. These results identify the most frequently occurring residues in the IDUA protein sequence.

### 3. How many potential N-glycosylation motifs did you find? Where are they located?

The number and positions of potential N-glycosylation motifs were determined by scanning the NP_000194.2 sequence for the N-X-S/T pattern, where X represents any standard amino acid except proline. The exact motif count, positions, and sequences are provided in the R-generated motif table. Previous structural research identified six N-linked glycosylation sites at N110, N190, N336, N372, N415, and N451 (Maita et al., 2013). These experimentally studied sites should be distinguished from motifs predicted by sequence analysis.

### 4. Why can glycosylation matter for lysosomal proteins? Does finding a motif prove the protein is glycosylated?

Glycosylation is important for many lysosomal proteins because it can contribute to proper folding, stability, intracellular transport, and enzyme activity. In IDUA, research has shown that the N-linked glycan attached at N372 contributes to substrate binding and catalytic function (Maita et al., 2013). However, finding an N-X-S/T motif does not prove that the protein is glycosylated at that position. It only indicates a potential site for N-linked glycosylation. Experimental evidence is necessary to confirm glycan attachment and determine its structure.

### 5. What could happen in the cell if your enzyme did not work properly?

If IDUA does not function properly, dermatan sulfate and heparan sulfate cannot be degraded normally and may accumulate inside lysosomes. This accumulation disrupts cellular function and affects multiple tissues and organs. IDUA deficiency causes mucopolysaccharidosis type I (MPS I), a lysosomal storage disorder that can cause skeletal abnormalities, organ enlargement, joint stiffness, and, in severe forms, progressive neurological problems. These effects demonstrate the importance of IDUA in normal lysosomal catabolism (Maita et al., 2013).
